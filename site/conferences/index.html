Impact of Social Media on Students' Performance in Academic Writing, Regional Dhaka University–IML Conference (2020)

Research Project Duration: July 2019 – December 2019

Affiliation: Department of English Language, Institute of Modern Languages (IML), University of Dhaka

The study was presented at the Regional Dhaka University–IML Conference (2020), where it contributed to ongoing scholarly discussions on the intersections of digital culture, language acquisition, and academic literacy. The presentation highlighted pedagogical implications for English language teaching, including the need to integrate digital literacy strategies into academic writing courses to bridge the gap between informal and formal writing practices.

This study examined the impact of social media engagement on the academic writing performance of university students. The project explored how frequent exposure to informal digital communication (e.g., Facebook, Twitter, messaging platforms) affected students’ vocabulary use, sentence structure, and overall writing proficiency in academic contexts. Adopting a mixed-methods approach, the study combined a survey of undergraduate students with a corpus-based analysis of writing samples. Data collection included questionnaires measuring social media usage patterns, writing assignments assessed through standardized rubrics, and interviews to capture student perceptions. The findings revealed both positive and negative trends: while social media fostered increased fluency, idea generation, and confidence in written expression, it also led to challenges in maintaining formal register, cohesion, and grammatical accuracy.

Research Title: Assessment of Technological Infrastructure, Teacher Competency, Systemic Agility, and Pedagogical Adaptability for Future Pandemic Readiness in HEIs

Executive Summary:

The COVID-19 pandemic precipitated an abrupt and largely unplanned shift from face-to-face instruction to online-based teaching and learning across the higher education sector. Institutions worldwide were compelled to move their courses online and adopt what has been termed emergency remote teaching (ERT) , a response that is conceptually and operationally distinct from the preplanned, purpose-built distance-education programs that preceded it. Whereas conventional distance education is designed in advance around known infrastructural and pedagogical parameters, ERT was improvised under conditions of acute uncertainty, and the transition demanded of teachers, students, and administrators not merely psychological endurance, but a combination of instructional resilience, technical competency, and institutional resourcing that many higher education institutions (HEIs) had not previously planned to develop.

A substantial body of research produced in the years following the onset of the pandemic examined how higher-education teachers navigated this transition. However, much of this literature has concentrated on the evolving role of the teacher within the Virtual Learning Environment (VLE) considered in isolation, rather than on how that role interacts with the technological infrastructure available to the institution, the systemic agility of the institution as an organization, and the pedagogical adaptability required at the classroom level. Studies in this vein have tended to describe the pressures placed on teachers to improvise interactive forms of instruction, while offering comparatively little empirical support on the institutional and infrastructural conditions that made such improvisation possible, difficult, or in some cases impossible. Within the context of Bangladesh specifically, competency in navigating a fully virtual teaching environment remains, for a considerable proportion of higher-education faculty, an unfamiliar undertaking, and the institutional response to the pandemic disruption has not yet been systematically documented or evaluated.

It is also worth noting that despite operating in an era of rapid technological change, decision-makers within many HEIs continue to equate technology integration in teaching with comparatively superficial applications, for instance, the use of presentation software with elaborate visual transitions, rather than with the deeper infrastructural, competency-based, and organizational capacities that determine whether an institution can sustain the quality of instruction under disruption. The existing literature, while distinguishing in general terms between the duties, roles, and competencies expected of teachers in virtual settings, has not consistently specified what is meant by "competency" in this context, nor delineated teachers' functions with the precision needed to inform training design. Many of the studies conducted to date have also been restricted to single-institution settings, which limits the extent to which their findings can be generalized for a global setting.

This projects responds to that gap by proposing to test, for the first time within a single empirical design, a four-construct framework of pandemic readiness for higher educational institutions (HEIs), comprising Technological Infrastructure, Teacher Competency, Systemic Agility, and Pedagogical Adaptability. Rather than examining teacher competency or technological infrastructure in isolation, as much of the prior literature has done, the proposed study is designed to examine how these four constructs relate to one another, so that the resulting evidence base can meaningfully inform how HEIs prepare for future disruptions of a health-related, ecological, or socio-political nature.

Research Title: English for Legal Practitioners: A Needs Analysis and Curriculum Redesign for the Legal Sector in Bangladesh

Executive Summary:

English for Specific Purposes (ESP) has increasingly gained prominence across professional domains, and the legal sector in Bangladesh is no exception. This study investigates the English language needs of legal practitioners in Bangladeshi courts, drawing on a mixed-methods design comprising surveys, semi-structured interviews, and Focus Group Discussions (FGDs). Findings reveal that legal vocabulary constitutes the primary barrier to effective English communication, while writing and speaking skills are identified as the most critical competence for professional performance, particularly at the High Court level. Practitioners rated their overall English proficiency as working-level, with accuracy and vocabulary acknowledged as key weaknesses. Dissatisfaction with prior English for Legal Purposes (ELP) courses is evident, followed by inadequate focus on practical legal language. The study concludes with a proposed revised ELP syllabus and recommendations for course developers, teacher trainers, and institutional policymakers.

Research Title: Domain-Authentic ESP Needs for Criminology, Law, and Armed-Forces Education: A Target-Situation and Learning-Needs Analysis for Corpus-Informed ESP Reform in HEIs

Executive Summary:

English for Specific Purposes (ESP) provision is now routine in criminology, armed-forces, and law curricula, yet little empirical evidence confirms whether such courses align with graduates' occupational communication demands in forensic, investigative, armed-forces, and legal-professional settings, particularly in South Asian higher education institutions (HEIs). Grounded in Munby's Communicative Needs Processor, Hutchinson and Waters' learning-centred approach to needs analysis, and Dudley-Evans and St John's framework for target-situation and learning-needs analysis, this study conceptualizes ESP curriculum relevance as the alignment between occupational communication demands, learners' present competencies, and instructional provision. This study reports a target-situation and learning-needs analysis of English communication demands among 1,296 students, graduates, and faculty (960 male, 336 female; age range 21--40 years, M = 26.9, SD = 5.65) drawn from Criminology and Armed Forces Studies (89%) and Law (11%) programs at HEIs in Bangladesh. A structured survey elicited self-rated proficiency, frequency of occupational English use, perceived target needs, skill-specific strengths and deficits, and curriculum adequacy. Although 59% reported using English frequently or always at work, 52% rated their proficiency as only "limited working," revealing a marked use--proficiency gap. Writing official documents (M = 4.56/5) and analytical reading (M = 4.30/5) emerged as the most strongly endorsed needs, exceeding interpersonal communication (M = 3.74/5), and 74% evaluated current course materials inadequate for their disciplinary needs. Respondents prioritized the integration of domain-authentic materials, e.g., forensic-report and case-file reading (67%) and English-news exposure (78--89%), over generic test-preparation resources such as IELTS/TOEFL/PTE practice (56%), and identified grammatical accuracy in writing and fluency in speaking as the most persistent skill gaps. These findings expose a systematic learning-situation mismatch in current provision and provide an empirical basis for corpus-informed ESP curriculum reform in criminology, armed-forces, and law education, with implications for the wider security-sector and armed-forces ESP landscape worldwide.

Research Title: Generative AI and Student Epistemic Agency: A Structural Investigation of Pedagogical Adaptability, Critical Academic Engagement, Technology Acceptance, Systemic Agility, and Equitable Integration Strategies for HEIs

Executive Summary:

The rapid proliferation of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) chatbots compels urgent structural scrutiny: the question is no longer whether students use them, but how, with what critical consciousness, and under what institutional conditions. This study examines structural interrelationships among five theoretically grounded constructs, i.e., technology acceptance, epistemic capability, critical academic engagement, systemic agility, and equitable integration, as determinants of student epistemic agency. A quantitative survey was administered to students (N = 1215) at a Higher Education Institution in the Global South, with items spanning GenAI utility, AI literacy, source verification, institutional support, academic integrity, and reflexive engagement across a six-point Likert scale. Construct-level analyses revealed a structurally significant asymmetry: individual-level constructs, i.e., technology acceptance (M = 3.53, SD = 0.74), epistemic capability (M = 3.81, SD = 0.72), critical academic engagement (M = 3.76, SD = 0.80), and equitable integration (M = 3.77, SD = 0.85) , demonstrated moderately strong profiles, with 96.3% affirming GenAI utility and 92.6% reporting AI-assisted reasoning as intellectually enriching. Conversely, systemic agility registered a markedly depressed mean (M = 2.65, SD = 1.18), exposing a critical institution–learner gap: fewer than half reported adequate technical support (44.4%), while barely over half indicated clear institutional guidance (53.1%) or a supportive AI-enabled community (55.6%). These findings reveal that students are epistemically ready for GenAI integration while institutions remain structurally unprepared, constituting systemic epistemic inequity.

Research Title: Constituent-Guided Lexical Prediction During Language Comprehension: A Neurocognitive Synthesis of Hierarchical Syntactic Structures and Its Transformative Implications for Education

Executive Summary:

Language comprehension is increasingly understood as a predictive process in which the brain anticipates upcoming input rather than passively decoding it. An unresolved question is the grain at which prediction operates: do comprehenders predict words primarily from linear word-to-word associations, or does the hierarchical, constituent-based structure of syntax guide lexical anticipation? This paper offers a neurocognitive synthesis of evidence bearing on constituent-guided lexical prediction, the proposal that syntactic phrase structure constrains and accelerates the prediction of upcoming words. Integrating findings from predictive-processing theory, electrophysiology (notably the N400 and related components), neural-oscillation research, and computational modeling, the investigator argues that prediction is structurally organised: constituents define the space of likely continuations, and the parser propagates structural expectations downward to the lexical level. The author reconciles linear-statistical and hierarchical accounts within a single framework in which both contribute but operate at different timescales and levels of representation. The investigator then translates this synthesis into education, arguing that reading instruction, second-language pedagogy, and assessment can be made more effective by explicitly cultivating learners' hierarchical structural sensitivity rather than treating prediction as mere word-association. The paper closes with a research agenda and concrete, testable instructional design principles. This synthesis is conceptual; empirical claims are attributed to the cited literature and require verification against the original sources.

Research Title: English as a Patient Safety Variable in Pharmacy and Medical Science: A Mixed-Method Needs Analysis and Curriculum Design Framework for Healthcare Professionals

Executive Summary:

Background. Patient safety, defined as the prevention of errors and adverse effects associated with healthcare delivery, is among the most consequential imperatives in contemporary biomedical practice. While a substantial body of clinical research has identified human factors, systemic failures, and procedural lapses as primary determinants of adverse healthcare events, the role of language, and specifically English language proficiency, as an independent, measurable patient safety variable has received disproportionately limited attention in the educational and health sciences literature. In pharmacy and medical science, where precision of expression is not merely a communicative virtue but a clinical imperative, the consequences of language inadequacy manifest with particular acuity: misread prescriptions, misinterpreted drug monographs, failed patient counseling, erroneous adverse event documentation, and breakdowns in inter-professional communication constitute a constellation of language-mediated risks whose frequency and severity remain systematically underestimated. Within postcolonial and developing healthcare systems, where English functions simultaneously as the dominant medium of scientific knowledge production, professional credentialing, and institutional documentation, yet frequently exists in asymmetric relationship to practitioners' first-language competencies, this gap in the scholarly and curricular record is not merely an academic oversight; it is a structural vulnerability with measurable implications for patient outcomes.

Purpose. This study investigates the specific English language needs of pharmacy and medical science professionals and trainees operating within healthcare environments where English functions as the primary medium of clinical documentation, drug information retrieval, inter-professional communication, and patient education. The study further aims to develop an evidence-based, empirically grounded curriculum design framework capable of systematically addressing identified language gaps in relation to patient safety competencies.

Methodology. The study employs a concurrent mixed-method design, integrating quantitative survey instrumentation with qualitative phenomenological inquiry to achieve methodological triangulation and conceptual depth. In the quantitative strand, a validated English language needs assessment instrument, developed through expert panel review, pilot testing, and Rasch model calibration, was administered to a purposively stratified sample of pharmacy students, pharmacy practitioners, undergraduate medical students, and junior clinical officers. Survey constructs operationalized four target-situation domains: pharmaceutical documentation and drug labeling literacy; patient counseling and informed consent communication; inter-professional clinical discourse; and adverse drug reaction reporting proficiency. In the qualitative strand, semi-structured interviews and focus group discussions were conducted with the same participant cohort, supplemented by interviews with clinical supervisors, pharmacy educators, and patient safety officers. Interview data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's six-phase protocol. Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings was achieved through joint display analysis at the interpretive synthesis stage, enabling discordant and convergent data patterns to mutually interrogate and enrich one another.

Findings. The integrated findings reveal a structurally embedded and professionally consequential English language deficit across all participant categories, manifesting differentially by professional context, institutional tier, and task domain. Quantitative analysis identifies statistically significant proficiency gaps in pharmaceutical package insert interpretation, adverse event narrative writing, and patient information leaflet comprehension, with the sharpest deficits concentrated among practitioners in public sector healthcare facilities and graduates of Bangla-medium undergraduate programs. Qualitative analysis surfaces three overarching thematic configurations: (i) professional linguistic camouflage, wherein practitioners deploy strategic avoidance, approximation, and peer-dependent workarounds to mask English language inadequacies in high-stakes clinical interactions, generating what participants describe as a persistent state of professional anxiety; (ii) curriculum-practice dissonance, wherein the English language instruction received during professional training is perceived as fundamentally misaligned with the linguistic demands of authentic clinical practice , oriented toward generic academic writing rather than genre-specific, task-authentic healthcare communication; and (iii) institutionalized silence on language risk, wherein healthcare organizations exhibit a systemic reluctance to identify, document, or address language-mediated error events, resulting in invisible but recurrent safety near-misses attributable to communicative breakdown. The joint display analysis further identifies a critical convergence point: practitioners who reported the lowest English proficiency in pharmaceutical documentation tasks were also those who reported the highest frequency of informal workaround behaviors, a pattern that the study theorizes as a language-mediated safety work-around cycle, representing a previously unarticulated mechanism of systemic risk amplification.

Research Title: Digital Inclusion and Equity in Higher Education: A Critical-Participatory Pedagogy for Inclusive Academic Engagement of the Digital Multitude

Executive Summary:

Democratization of digital technology in higher education institutions (HEIs) has become increasingly complicated by evidence of systemic injustices that limit the academic engagement of the digital multitude. Despite the unprecedented growth of educational technology in tertiary level institutions, persistent asymmetries in digital access, institutional recognition, and pedagogical responsiveness continue to marginalize large and diverse segments of the student body, exposing fundamental inadequacies in prevailing frameworks for digital inclusion and equity. This mixed methods study draws on critical pedagogy and participatory action to explore the structural and pedagogical mechanisms of digital inclusion and equity in HEIs. A convergent parallel mixed-methods design was used with: (i) a Systematic Literature Review (SLR; N=51 studies) performed according to the PRISMA 2020 protocol with binary Quality Assessment Rule (QAR) scoring; (ii) a structured cross-institutional survey (N=418 faculty and students from six HEIs) analyzed with Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) and Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) with mediation analysis; and (iii) semi-structured individual interviews (N=30 participants) and Focus Group Discussions (FGDs; k=4 groups, n=38) analyzed with inductive thematic analysis. Psychometric validation revealed good concept reliability (Cronbach's α range: .82–.93; CR > .86; AVE > .52) across four latent constructs Academic Engagement. The results of SEM demonstrated namely Digital Infrastructure Access, Institutional Equity Governance, Critical-Participatory Pedagogy and Inclusive that the Critical-Participatory Pedagogy strongly mediated the association between Digital Infrastructure Access and Inclusive Academic Engagement (β = .44, p < .001) and institutional equity governance considerably moderated this relation (β = .31, p < .05). Inductive thematic analysis produced six superordinate themes that represent the experience of digital exclusion and participatory capital formation in the digital multitude. The findings have implications for the HEI policy, the design of pedagogical curricula, the institutional digital governance, and the systemic transformation of inclusive academic engagement.

Research Title: Attitudes Toward Animation-Based Instruction (ABI): A Multilevel Investigation of Adoption, Cognitive Engagement, Affective Orientations, Pedagogical Efficacy, and Academic Outcomes in Primary Classrooms

Executive Summary:

Although the theoretical basis for the use of animation-based instruction (ABI) is established in the Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning and the Technology Acceptance Model, researchers’ attitudinal work has been limited to the use of unidimensional operationalisation, single-level analysis, and high resource contexts, which obscures the multilevel mechanisms that link teacher disposition to student outcomes. Drawing on CTML, TAM, the tripartite attitude model, and Self-Efficacy Theory, this study is the first to investigate the ABI adoption intention, cognitive engagement, affective orientations, pedagogical efficacy, and academic achievement in Bangladeshi primary EFL classrooms at multiple levels simultaneously. A sequential explanatory mixed-methods design was used: a cross-sectional multilevel survey with a quasi-experimental pretest–posttest component (N = 270 teachers; N ≈ 5,400 Grade III–V students; 90 government primary schools).

Research Title: Digital Literacy and Self-Efficacy of English Language Teachers in South Asia

Executive Summary:

Pedagogical interventions using machine-generated language have advanced sufficiently to investigate the current efficacies and competencies of practitioners responsible for classroom instruction. This study examines the intersection of digital literacy and instructional self-efficacy among English language teachers in South Asia. In an era of expanding educational technology, it addresses how teachers’ technological proficiency relates to their confidence and teaching effectiveness. Employing an integrative literature review and thematic analysis, the research synthesizes findings from diverse studies on technology integration, teacher training, and digital skill development in South Asian classrooms. The analysis reveals a strong positive association between teachers’ digital competencies and their self-efficacy. The review notes significant variation in proficiency: younger or recently trained educators often demonstrate stronger digital fluency, while others may rely mainly on basic skills. Educators with higher proficiency in digital tools,from basic productivity software to advanced content creation and collaboration platforms,report greater confidence in integrating technology into language instruction. This, in turn, leads to more innovative, engaging, and effective teaching practices. Conversely, shortcomings in advanced digital skills, limited access to reliable infrastructure, and inadequate institutional support often undermine teacher confidence and impede the adoption of new pedagogical tools. The study concludes that overcoming these barriers requires structured, ongoing professional development and context-specific integration of technology. It underscores the importance of tailored training programs and supportive institutional policies that align with the actual needs of language teachers. By reinforcing teachers’ digital capacities through targeted resources and sustained support, education systems can empower educators to leverage technology in pedagogically meaningful ways. Ultimately, these measures contribute to closing the digital divide and enhancing language learning outcomes across the region.

Research Title: Emotional Intelligence and Academic Performance: The Mediating Role of Academic Engagement

Executive Summary:

This study aims to investigate the impact of emotional intelligence (EI) on academic performance (AP) through the mediation effect of academic engagement (AE). The research was conducted as a pilot study of the researchers’ main project among students in the Bangladeshi higher educational institutions. The pilot data were collected from 62 Master’s level students selected from the Faculty of Business Studies and Faculty of Science of Mawlana Bhashani Science and Technology University. A self-administered questionnaire was used for gathering data, and SPSS 25 and SmartPLS 4.0 software were employed to analyze the data. To test the hypothesized relationships, the study employed Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Model (PLS-SEM) approach. The results revealed that EI does not have a direct effect on AP (β=0.062); however, it exerts an indirect influence through AE (β=0.176), indicating a full mediation effect of AE. The variance (R2 ) of EI and AE on AP is 33.5%. Furthermore, the findings confirmed that EI significantly influences AE (β=0.316) with a variance (R2 ) of 10%, and AE significantly predicts AP (β=0.556). The implications of these findings suggest that enhancing students’ EI and AE can meaningfully improve their academic outcomes. The study contributes to the growing body of literature on EI, AE, and AP. Although the results may not be fully generalizable due to the pilot nature and limited sample size, they provide valuable insights and directions for future research in the context of Bangladeshi higher education.

Research Title: English Language Assessment Policies and Pedagogical Realities in Bangladeshi Secondary Education

Executive Summary:

Assessment plays a critical role in educational systems, serving as a source of information on the achievement of learning goals and as a basis for important decisions on student evaluation, curriculum, and teaching methods. This study investigates the alignment between declared policies and actual practices of English language assessment in secondary schools of Dhaka, Bangladesh. The findings reveal a great discrepancy between the recommended assessment policies, which call for a comprehensive assessment of the four language skills (reading, writing, listening and speaking) and the common classroom practices where the assessment primarily centers around reading and writing, largely ignoring listening and speaking skills. This difference is further aggravated by such factors as large class size, insufficient training of teachers in communicative assessment techniques, and limited availability of multimedia resources. The paper concludes with strategic recommendations to bridge this policy-practice gap, including increased teacher professional development, integration of all four skills into formal assessment frameworks, and provision of essential technological infrastructure to foster a more holistic and effective English language learning environment in Bangladesh.

Research Title: Impact of Multiple Intelligence on English Language Classroom in Tertiary Education

Executive Summary:

Over the past four decades, Gardner (1985) started hypothesizing about multiple intelligences (MI). In other words, research concerning MI theory in tertiary education is exiguous. To encourage language learning in classrooms through tasks that complement individual learner's interests and strengths, this paper reports the roles of multiple intelligence in English language classrooms in tertiary education. MI-based programs, including approaches and activities, are used to demonstrate how MI theory includes various language classifications. Other data research has examined whether ELT practitioners view Multiple Intelligence as a classroom technique. This paper finally inspects any specific barrier in the way to consider Multiple Intelligences in English language classrooms. In this paper, the researcher has provided a profile of multiple intelligences of university students, which may foster their learning processes in English language classrooms in tertiary education. The research is qualitative in nature. In order to collect data, the researcher has interviewed five English language teachers who are currently teaching tertiary level students. Besides, the researcher also conducted a focus group discussion with twenty undergraduate students. The researcher observed a thematic data analysis. All the teachers said that they practice multiple intelligences in their classes, and they think that classes considering multiple intelligences are facilitating for English Language learners. The barriers the teachers face to conduct the classes considering multiple intelligences in the classroom are the absence of formal raining, lack of technological devices, classroom settings, lack of up-to-the-mark materials, etc. Majority of the students claimed that they have the experience of multiple intelligence integrated classes and courses. They expressed their different preferred intelligence. The common intelligence was visual-spatial intelligence. They faced difficulties in the classes where the teachers used only the linguistic verbal intelligence. To overcome the barriers in order to conduct the classes considering multiple intelligence, the teachers suggest to train the teachers on the application of multiple intelligence in the English language classrooms, provide audio-visual equipment and other technological devices, and make the classroom setting comfortable and suitable, develop the instructional materials for the application of multiple intelligences in the English language classrooms.

Research Title: Technology in English Language Teaching and Learning at Tertiary Level Education in Bangladesh: Challenges and Possibilities

Executive Summary:

The purpose of this research is to investigate the challenges and possibilities of technology in developing the English language skills of Bangladeshi tertiary level students. Thus, the research aims to explore whether or not English Language teachers view technology-enhanced language learning and teaching as useful methods as they are popularized, at the outset of the research. The study will then investigate the perceptions of teachers and students regarding technology, how teachers integrate technology in the classroom, the obstacles to integrating technology, and teachers’ recommendations for devising an effective technology integration plan. The literature review indicates that technology enhances learners' cognitive ability, promotes customized learning, increases interactivity with peers and materials, fosters self-direction among learners, creates a learner-centered atmosphere etc. However, there is a lack of research on the effectiveness of technology in English Language Teaching and Learning in Bangladesh. There is a lack of evidence describing which technological instruments are effective for developing students' specific language skills. To provide insights and in-depth data for this research, the study will observe a qualitative-quantitative methodology using interviews with teachers and students, and focus group discussions with undergraduate students and, tertiary level classroom observations. The data will be thematically analyzed and the results will be used to devise a plan for the integration of technology into English Language Teaching and learning in Bangladesh.
